The ML Algorithm Toolbox — The AI You Don't See

What decided that fraud alert, that loan approval, and which model answered your last prompt?​

A charge on your card gets flagged as fraud before you even notice it happened. A loan application gets an instant yes or no. A request to an AI product gets routed to a small, cheap model for a simple question and a bigger, slower one for a hard question. None of those decisions runs on a neural network the size of ChatGPT. They run on a small set of algorithms that are older, cheaper, and — for the kind of structured, row-and-column data a bank or a product team actually has — often better.

This module opens that toolbox. You will meet five algorithms you can name and reason about by the end of the lesson, and one of them, a single neuron, turns out to compute the exact same math as every word an LLM produces. By the time Module 5 opens the LLM itself, you will already know the arithmetic running inside it.

The toolbox, end to end​

Five algorithms, one thread connecting them: each one is a slightly more capable way of doing the same basic job — take some inputs, weigh them, and produce an answer. Follow that thread and the neuron at the end stops looking mysterious.

Keep this shape in mind. Trees and forests are one branch, and that branch still runs the tabular data your bank, your fraud team, and your ranking systems depend on. Logistic regression and the neuron are the other branch, and that branch is where this module's biggest payoff lives.

Linear regression: the model you already built​

You already built one of these. Module 3's lab trained a model to predict a house's price from its square footage, bedrooms, and location — that model was a linear regression. It gives every feature a weight, multiplies each feature by its weight, adds the results up, and adds one more constant on top. Adjust the weights until that sum lands close to the real price across thousands of examples, and you have a working predictor.

Why you care beyond the lab: that weighted-sum idea — feature times weight, summed up — is the arithmetic core that every other algorithm in this module reuses in some form. Learn it once here and you will keep recognizing it.

Logistic regression: the logit an LLM computes for every word​

Analogy: picture a loan officer working from a scorecard. Income adds points, a long credit history adds points, existing debt subtracts points. Add them all up and you get one raw score. A linear regression would stop right there and hand you that score as a plain number. A loan decision needs something else — not "your score is 340," but "an 82% chance of approval."

Logistic regression runs the loan officer's weighted sum exactly like linear regression does, then passes that raw sum through a squashing function called sigmoid, which bends any number, however large or small, into a value between 0 and 1 — a probability. That raw sum, before the squashing, has a name: the logit. Remember that word, because here is the payoff of this whole section: an LLM computes a logit for every single word it could say next. For each candidate word in its vocabulary, it runs the same weighted-sum arithmetic you just saw in the loan officer's scorecard, gets one raw logit per candidate, then squashes the whole set of them — this time with sigmoid's multi-option cousin, softmax — into a probability distribution over every word it could pick. Then it samples one. Predicting the next word in a sentence and approving a loan application run on the same underlying math: logistic regression, just aimed at a vocabulary of tens of thousands of words instead of two outcomes.

Decision trees: a flowchart that learns itself​

Analogy: think about a helpdesk troubleshooting flowchart. Is it plugged in? If no, plug it in. If yes, is the outlet working? Each answer sends you down a different branch until you land on an actual fix.

A decision tree is that flowchart, except the questions ("is income above X?", "is this transaction amount above Y?") and the order they get asked in are learned from data, not written by a person. At each step, the tree finds the single split of the data that separates the outcomes best, asks that question, and repeats on each half, until it reaches a leaf — a final prediction. The result is a model you can trace, question by question, to see exactly why it made the call it made.

Random forests and boosting: why tabular data still belongs to trees​

One tree overfits easily. Let it ask enough narrow, specific questions and it will happily memorize your training data instead of the real pattern — Module 3's overfitting problem, showing up again. Two fixes for that, and both are still built from trees.

A random forest trains many trees, each one on a random slice of the data and a random subset of the features, then averages their answers. No single tree gets the final say. It is a panel of judges, not one judge, so an outlier in any one tree gets outvoted by the rest.

Boosting — the idea behind tools like XGBoost and LightGBM — builds trees one after another instead of in parallel, and each new tree is trained specifically to fix the mistakes the ones before it made. It is a chain of editors, where each pass focuses only on what the last pass got wrong.

Why you care: on rows-and-columns data, boosted trees and random forests still beat neural networks on accuracy most of the time, and they need far less data to get there. That is why fraud detection systems, search and ad ranking, and the router that decides which model answers your prompt inside a multi-model AI product are usually running a forest or a boosted tree, not a neural network. Neural networks earn their keep on images, audio, and text — raw, unstructured signal. Structured business data is still tree territory.

The neuron: one weighted decision, stacked into a network​

Look back at logistic regression's shape: a weighted sum, then a squashing function. That is also, exactly, what a single neuron computes. Give a neuron some inputs, and it multiplies each one by a weight, adds a bias, and passes the total through an activation function — sigmoid is one choice, but a simpler one called ReLU (it just outputs the number itself when positive, and zero otherwise) is more common in practice. One neuron, by itself, is a logistic regression wearing a different name.

The part that matters is what happens when you stack them. Take a layer of neurons, feed every input to every one of them, and feed all of their outputs forward as the inputs to the next layer. Each layer learns to combine what the layer before it noticed into something a little more abstract — early layers respond to simple patterns, later layers combine those into something closer to the actual answer you want. That stack is a neural network, and it is the one piece of this toolbox that keeps scaling: add more layers, more neurons per layer, more training data, and it keeps getting more capable in a way a single tree or a single logistic regression does not. Every model you touch for the rest of this course — today's digit classifier, and every LLM from Module 5 onward — is this same stack of weighted sums and squashing functions, just far bigger. This is the substrate of everything that follows.

Which algorithm do I use?​

Your data looks like...Reach forWhy
Rows and columns, predict a numberLinear regressionSimple, fast, and a strong first baseline
Rows and columns, predict yes/no or a categoryLogistic regressionFast, interpretable, and gives you a real probability
Rows and columns, need to explain the exact reasoningA decision treeYou can trace the decision, question by question
Rows and columns, need the best accuracy you can getRandom forest or boostingUsually wins on tabular data — fraud, ranking, routing
Images, audio, raw textA neural networkLearns its own features from unstructured signal

What's next​

You now know the arithmetic sitting under logistic regression and a single neuron: a weighted sum, then a squashing function, turning raw scores into probabilities. Module 5 takes that exact same logit and scales it up into a full language model — tokens, transformers, and the sampling step that turns a distribution of logits into the next word on your screen.
