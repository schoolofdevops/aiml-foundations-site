How Machines Actually Learn — The Ideas Inside ChatGPT

How did ChatGPT learn to write, and why doesn't it learn from your chats?​

You already know from Module 2 that an LLM predicts the next chunk of text, one token at a time. But you type a question and it types back an answer, and if you correct it mid-conversation, it seems to adjust. So why does the same model give you the exact same wrong answer again tomorrow, in a brand-new conversation, as if today never happened?

Because everything a model knows was decided once, during training, long before you opened a chat window. What happens inside a conversation is a different process called inference, and the model cannot rewire itself while that's happening. To see why, and to see where hallucination and API cost actually come from, you need three ideas: how a machine learns a pattern from data, what happens once training stops, and what goes wrong when the learning itself is bad. This module builds all three, with a real predictor you train yourself.

Three ways a machine learns​

Before a model can predict, generate, or act, it has to learn a pattern from data — and there are exactly three ways to hand it that pattern. Every modern AI system scales one of these three up to enormous size, so once you name the pattern in a small example, you'll recognize it inside ChatGPT too.

Supervised learning​

Analogy: imagine training a new hire by handing them ten thousand past support tickets, each already marked with the resolution that actually worked. They read ticket after ticket until they start recognizing the pattern themselves — a similar complaint comes in next week, and they know roughly what to do, even though they never saw that exact ticket before.

That's supervised learning: you show the model many examples, each paired with the correct answer (the label), and it learns the mapping between the two. Predicting a house's sale price from its square footage, bedrooms, and location is supervised learning — the labels are past sale prices you already know.

Why you care: this is literally how LLMs are pre-trained and fine-tuned. Pre-training feeds the model billions of sentences where the "label" for each chunk of text is simply the next chunk — predict what comes next, check it against what actually came next, adjust. Fine-tuning does the same thing on a smaller, curated set of examples of the exact behavior you want.

Unsupervised learning​

Analogy: now imagine handing that same new hire ten thousand customer records with no categories attached — no "high value," no "at risk," nothing. Left alone, they'd still start noticing groups: customers who buy the same three products together, customers who only shop during a sale. Nobody told them the groups existed. They found the structure themselves.

That's unsupervised learning: there are no labels, so the model looks for structure in the data on its own — which points naturally cluster together, which sit far apart. Grouping customers by purchase behavior for a marketing campaign, with no predefined segments handed to the algorithm, is a classic case.

Why you care: this becomes embeddings, the M2 term for turning text into a point in space, positioned so similar meanings land close together. That's unsupervised structure-finding, run on language instead of purchase histories — the technology underneath RAG and semantic search finding the right document without you typing its exact words.

Reinforcement learning​

Analogy: think about training a dog with treats and corrections. Nobody shows the dog a labeled dataset of "correct sits." It tries something, gets a reward or doesn't, and adjusts what it tries next time. Repeat that enough times and the behavior you wanted starts to stick.

That's reinforcement learning: an agent takes an action, gets a reward or a penalty as feedback, and gradually learns which actions lead to more reward — no pre-labeled correct answer for every situation, just consequences, tried and adjusted over and over.

Why you care: this is the RLHF step — reinforcement learning from human feedback — that turned a raw text-predictor into something that answers helpfully and refuses harmful requests. A model with only supervised pre-training would still just predict plausible next text; RLHF is the reward-and-penalty pass that shapes those predictions into a model worth talking to.

Features and the train/test split​

For any of these three to work, the machine needs data broken into features: the individual measurable inputs it learns from — square footage, bedrooms, and location for a house, or purchase frequency and order size for a customer. Features are the columns; each row is one example.

Here's a mistake that quietly ruins a model's credibility: training it and testing it on the same data. Study for an exam using the actual questions and you'll score near 100 percent — proving nothing about whether you learned the subject. The fix is a train/test split: hold back a portion of your data the model never sees during training, then use it afterward to check performance on examples it genuinely never saw. You'll make this exact split in today's lab, and the gap between your training score and your test score is the most honest number you'll get out of the model.

Training vs inference — why the model doesn't learn from your chats​

Training is the phase where the model actually adjusts itself: it sees examples, compares its guess to the right answer, and updates its internal numbers to do a little better next time. This happens once, offline, over hours, days, or weeks of compute.

Inference is every time you use the trained model afterward: input in, output out, using the exact same fixed numbers it finished training with. Nothing you say during inference changes those numbers. That's the answer to this lesson's opening question — your conversation with ChatGPT is entirely inference. It can look like it's "learning" your correction within one conversation, because that correction is sitting right there in the context window it can read from, but the moment the conversation ends, the model is exactly as it was before you typed a word, ready for the next person with the numbers unchanged.

This also explains why an API call costs money. Training is one large, one-time cost the provider absorbs. Inference is what you're actually paying for, per call, measured in the tokens (M2's term) you send in and get back — every message is a fresh, metered pass through a model that retains nothing from the last one.

When learning goes wrong: overfitting, underfitting, and bias-variance​

Overfitting is what happens when a model learns the training data too well, including its noise and quirks, instead of the general pattern underneath. Picture a student who memorizes last year's exact exam paper, word for word — they'll ace a repeat of that paper and fail almost any real exam that asks the same material a different way. Underfitting is the opposite failure: the model never learned enough of the pattern, and performs poorly even on the data it trained on — that same student, having barely opened the textbook, does badly on both the practice exam and the real one.

Between these two sits the bias-variance trade-off: a model with high bias is too simple and underfits, missing real patterns; a model with high variance is too sensitive and overfits, treating noise as signal. You'll watch that trade-off show up as real numbers in today's lab.

Why you care: this is the root cause of hallucination (M2's term). A model that overfit its training data, or never saw enough examples covering a topic, has no real pattern to fall back on once you ask it something outside what it learned well — so it generates the most statistically plausible-sounding text anyway, confident tone included, whether or not it's true. Hallucination isn't a separate, mysterious bug bolted onto language models. It's overfitting and underfitting, at internet scale, showing up as a sentence instead of a wrong number.
