Deep Dive — Attention, Embeddings Geometry, and Sampling Internals

The lab called an API and printed one generated answer. Part 2 opens up three pieces of the machinery that produced it: the arithmetic behind attention (the lesson gave you the intuition — here's a real worked example), what it actually means for two embeddings to be "close together" in the vector space RAG's retrieve step searches, and why the exact same question, sent twice, can come back with two different answers even against the exact same model.

Where this picks up

This page needs nothing from the lab's venv — every script below is plain Python 3 from the standard library (math, random), no packages, no network calls, nothing to configure. Run it from anywhere; re-running is always safe.

cd labs/m5

1 — Attention, worked by hand​

The lesson's contract-reading analogy said attention is a weighted glance back at every earlier token. Here is that glance, computed.

Take the sentence from the lesson: "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." A real transformer represents "it" as a query vector, and every earlier token as a key vector; the model computes a raw score — a dot product between the query and each key — then squashes those scores into weights with softmax, the exact same squashing function that turns raw logits into probabilities.

Using the lesson's numbers: score(it, trophy) = 0.90, score(it, suitcase) = 0.20. Softmax turns those two raw scores into weights that sum to 1:

weight(trophy)   = e^0.90 / (e^0.90 + e^0.20) ≈ 0.668
weight(suitcase) = e^0.20 / (e^0.90 + e^0.20) ≈ 0.332

"It" ends up represented as roughly two-thirds trophy, one-third suitcase — not a hard choice, a weighted blend, computed fresh for every token in every sentence. Change the last word to "small" instead of "big," and a real model's learned weights would shift those two raw scores the other way — "it" now leans toward "suitcase" — without anyone hand-coding that rule. The rule came entirely from the pattern the model saw across its training data.

This is the mechanism behind why an LLM handles long, ambiguous, pronoun-heavy documents better than older architectures: every token gets to look at every other token directly, at every layer, instead of the information having to survive being passed word-by-word down a chain.

Why this matters when a model gets a reference wrong

When an LLM confidently mixes up which entity a pronoun or a follow-up question refers to in a long document, this is the exact mechanism that failed — the attention weights landed on the wrong earlier token. It's the same failure mode as a person skimming too fast and grabbing the wrong antecedent, just happening inside the math instead of inside a person's read-through.

2 — Embeddings geometry: what "close together" actually means​

Module 2 defined an embedding as a point in space where similar meanings land close together. The lesson's RAG section used that to explain retrieval. Here's the actual measurement that "close together" means.

Analogy: picture a compass bearing instead of a straight-line distance. Two ships a kilometre apart but heading the same direction are, for navigation purposes, more "aligned" than two ships sitting right next to each other but pointed in opposite directions. Embedding similarity works on the same idea — it usually cares about direction, not raw distance.

The standard measurement is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between two vectors, computed as their dot product divided by the product of their lengths. It ranges from -1 (opposite meaning) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (same meaning). Two embeddings for "dog" and "puppy" sit at a small angle from each other — cosine similarity close to 1 — while "dog" and "invoice" sit at close to a right angle — cosine similarity near 0. Dividing by each vector's length is what makes this a measure of direction, not magnitude: a long, repetitive document chunk and a short, focused one can still point the same direction in meaning space, and cosine similarity treats them as equally relevant if they do.

This is precisely what RAG's retrieve step runs, once per chunk, against your query: rank every chunk by cosine similarity to the question's embedding, and keep the highest-scoring ones. A weak RAG answer is very often a retrieval problem, not a generation problem — the right chunk existed in your documents, but its embedding just didn't land close enough to the query's to get pulled into the top results.

3 — Sampling internals: why the same prompt gives different answers​

Run the sampling demo — it stands in one model's next-token decision (five toy candidate words with fixed scores) for a real one (tens of thousands of candidate tokens):

python3 sampling_demo.py

Expected output

temperature = 0.2
    sunny: 0.9933
   cloudy: 0.0067
    rainy: 0.0000
    windy: 0.0000
    snowy: 0.0000
  10 samples: ['sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny']

temperature = 1.0
    sunny: 0.6875
   cloudy: 0.2529
    rainy: 0.0342
    windy: 0.0208
    snowy: 0.0046
  10 samples: ['sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'cloudy', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'rainy', 'sunny', 'sunny']

temperature = 2.0
    sunny: 0.4795
   cloudy: 0.2908
    rainy: 0.1070
    windy: 0.0833
    snowy: 0.0394
  10 samples: ['snowy', 'sunny', 'rainy', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'sunny', 'rainy', 'sunny', 'cloudy']

top-p = 0.9 filter at temperature = 1.0
  kept candidates: ['sunny (0.6875)', 'cloudy (0.2529)']
  excluded from sampling entirely: ['rainy', 'snowy', 'windy']

Read the three temperature = blocks top to bottom. Temperature divides every raw score before softmax runs. At a low temperature, the gaps between scores get stretched apart, so the softmax step (Section 1's exact squashing function) produces a distribution that's almost entirely weight on the top candidate — the ten samples drawn from it come back nearly identical every time. At a high temperature, the same scores get compressed together, the distribution flattens across more candidates, and the ten samples drawn from it come back visibly more varied. That's the entire mechanism behind "the same prompt gave a different answer this time" — nothing random happened to the model's knowledge; the sampling step after the model's scoring is where the randomness lives, and temperature is the dial that controls how much of it there is.

Top-p (nucleus sampling) works differently: instead of reshaping every score, it ranks candidates by probability and keeps only the smallest set whose probabilities add up to your chosen threshold — the script's p=0.9 — discarding the long tail entirely before a single token gets sampled. Look at the script's last section: at temperature = 1.0, top-p keeps only the highest-probability candidates and excludes the rest from the sampling pool completely, no matter how many low-probability candidates that tail contained. Temperature reshapes the whole distribution; top-p trims which part of it is even eligible to be picked.

Where you will use this
  • A "creative" or "varied" output setting in an API or a product is temperature, not magic. Use it when: you're debugging why a support-bot's canned-sounding answers suddenly got erratic, or vice versa — check what the caller set temperature to before assuming the model itself changed.
  • Cosine similarity is the actual ranking behind "search this over your documents." Use it when: a RAG-backed assistant misses an answer that's clearly in the source documents — check the retrieval step and its similarity scores before assuming the generation step, or the model, is at fault.
  • Attention explains why longer, messier context sometimes degrades an answer — more tokens means more candidates competing for the same limited attention weight on any one of them. Use it when: an answer gets noticeably worse after you stuff in a large, loosely-relevant document instead of a short, targeted one — the fix is usually better retrieval or chunking, not a bigger context window.
