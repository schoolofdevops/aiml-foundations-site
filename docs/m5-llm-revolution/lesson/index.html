The LLM Revolution — Tokens, Transformers, RAG & Reasoning

Why did 2022 change everything?​

Machine learning has existed for decades. Neural networks are older still. So why did one release — ChatGPT, in November 2022 — turn "AI" into a word everyone at work suddenly used? You already have half the answer from Module 3: an LLM's pre-training is supervised learning, run at a scale nobody had reached before. This module gives you the other half — the transformer architecture that made training on close to the entire internet practical, plus what's built on top of it: prompting, retrieval, reasoning modes. These are what turn a raw text predictor into something you call from your own code.

By the end of this lesson, you can explain what a token and a parameter actually are, why attention drives everything an LLM does, where hallucination really comes from, how RAG grounds an answer in your own documents, and when to reach for prompting, RAG, or fine-tuning.

Tokens and parameters, one level deeper​

Module 2 defined a token as the small chunk of text a model reads and writes, and a parameter as one of the numbers a model learned during training. Here's the part that matters once you start calling a real API: a token is rarely a whole word. Feed a tokenizer the word "disconnecting" and it typically comes back as three or four pieces — something like dis, connect, ing — because the model was trained on subword units, not a fixed dictionary of whole words. A fixed vocabulary of whole words would miss every word it had never seen before; a small set of subword pieces can rebuild almost any word, including ones invented after training, by combining pieces it already knows. That's also why token counts surprise people — "disconnecting" costs three or four tokens, not one, and every one of those is billed and eats into your context window.

Parameters scale differently: a 70-billion-parameter model has 70 billion learned weights, each one a knob tuned during training. More parameters generally means more capacity to represent patterns — and a bigger compute bill, whether you're training it or just running it.

Transformers and attention: how a model reads a whole sentence at once​

Analogy: picture reading a contract and hitting the word "it" three paragraphs in. You don't read "it" on its own — you glance back over the page and weigh which earlier noun it's actually pointing at, giving more weight to the candidates that make sense given everything else you've read.

That glance-back-and-weigh step is what attention computes, for every token, against every other token around it. Take the sentence "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." When the model processes "it," attention scores it against every earlier word — say, 0.90 against "trophy" and 0.20 against "suitcase" — then squashes those scores into weights that sum to 1: about 67% trophy, 33% suitcase. The word "it" ends up represented mostly as "trophy," which is exactly the reading a person gives that sentence.

A transformer is a stack of layers built almost entirely from this operation, run in parallel across every token in the input at once — no word waits for the one before it to finish processing, the way an older recurrent network required. That parallelism is the real reason a transformer can train on close to the entire internet in a practical amount of time: you can throw thousands of chips at the same layer simultaneously, instead of working through one word after another.

Pre-training, fine-tuning, and RLHF — Module 3's ideas, at internet scale​

You already know supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning from Module 3. LLMs use all three, in sequence.

Pre-training is supervised learning with a label that costs nothing to produce: given a chunk of text, predict the next token, then check it against the token that actually came next. Every sentence on the internet supplies its own label this way, which is why pre-training can run on close to the entire written web without a human annotating anything.

Fine-tuning is the same supervised process, run again on a smaller, hand-curated set of examples that show the exact behavior you want — a format, an instruction-following style, a refusal rule.

RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback — is Module 3's third idea, applied to the model's own answers. Human raters rank sample responses; a reward model learns to predict those rankings; the LLM is nudged, reward and penalty, toward answers that score well. A model with only pre-training just predicts plausible next text. RLHF is the pass that shapes that raw predictor into something that follows instructions and stays on topic.

Prompt and context engineering, 2026 practice​

Once a model is trained, everything you do at call time is prompt engineering: a system prompt sets the model's role and rules for the whole conversation ("You are a support assistant; answer only from the provided documents"); few-shot examples show it two or three worked input/output pairs right inside the prompt; structured output asks for a specific JSON schema back, turning free text into something your code can parse without a fragile regex. Context engineering is the wider discipline — deciding what goes into the context window at all, since every token in there, system prompt, examples, retrieved documents, history, competes for the model's attention and your budget.

Why LLMs hallucinate — the real reason​

Module 2 named hallucination: a model stating something false with the same confident tone as something true. Module 3 gave you the real mechanism without naming it — overfitting and underfitting.

Here's the connection, made explicit. At every token, an LLM has no choice but to output a probability distribution over its whole vocabulary and pick one — there is no built-in "I don't know" unless training specifically shaped one in. Ask it something its training data covered thinly, or that falls outside the pattern it actually generalized, and it still has to emit something. It generates the most statistically plausible next token anyway, confident tone included, because generating fluent text and generating true text are different skills, and only one of them is what next-token prediction directly trains.

The one question that catches most hallucinations

Before trusting an answer that cites a fact, a number, or a source, ask: would the model have seen enough real examples of this specific thing to have actually learned it, or is it producing the most fluent-sounding guess? That single question catches more hallucinations than any amount of arguing with the model about its own confidence.

RAG: chunk, embed, retrieve, stuff, generate​

Module 2 introduced RAG in one paragraph. Here's the actual pipeline.

Analogy: think about the difference between grep-ing a codebase for an exact string and asking a colleague who's read the whole codebase where the relevant logic lives. grep only finds text that matches the letters you typed. Your colleague finds the right file even if you described it in completely different words, because they matched on meaning, not spelling. RAG gives a model that same colleague-level search over your own documents.

Chunk: split your source documents into small pieces — a paragraph or a few hundred tokens each — because embedding an entire manual as one vector would blur every fact in it into one average meaning. Embed: turn each chunk into an embedding, Module 2's term for a point in space where similar meanings sit close together. Retrieve: embed the incoming question the same way, then pull back the chunks whose vectors sit closest to it. Stuff: paste those chunks straight into the prompt's context window. Generate: the model answers from that grounded text instead of purely from what it memorized — exactly why a RAG-backed assistant can cite your own, current documents instead of guessing from a training cutoff.

Reasoning models: what "thinking" costs you​

A reasoning model spends extra tokens working through a problem step by step before it commits to a final answer — sometimes shown as a visible chain of steps, sometimes hidden and just billed. Why you care: switching "thinking mode" on for a simple lookup is pure waste of money and latency, while switching it off for a genuinely multi-step problem — planning, multi-hop reasoning, debugging a tricky failure — trades a wrong fast answer for a slower, more reliable one. It's a cost-versus-quality decision you make per request, not a setting you pick once and forget.

Decision guide: prompting vs RAG vs fine-tuning​

ApproachBest whenWhat it costsWhat it can't fix
Prompting aloneThe model already knows the topic; you're shaping tone, format, or focusCheapest — just the tokens per callKnowledge the model never saw in training
RAGThe model needs to answer from your own, current, or private documentsA chunk + embed + retrieve pipeline, plus retrieved text added to every callWeak retrieval — bad chunking or a bad match still produces a bad answer
Fine-tuningYou need one specific behavior or format baked in, at volume, without repeating instructions every callThe most expensive and slowest — a training run, curated examples, redone whenever the behavior must changeMissing knowledge — fine-tuning changes behavior, not facts; it still needs RAG for anything that changes after training
