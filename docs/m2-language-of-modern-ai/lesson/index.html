The Language of Modern AI — A Living Lexicon

Why does every AI product page sound like a different language?​

Read any AI release note from the last year: "context window expanded to 200K tokens," "now supports tool-calling," "hallucination rate down 30% on our internal eval," "same parameter count, better answers." Every one of those sentences is built from plain English words, wired together in a way that tells you nothing unless you already know what the words mean in this field. You're not missing intelligence or experience — you're missing about ten words.

This module hands you those ten words: the ones that show up in almost every AI product page, release note, and vendor pitch you'll read from here on. By the end, sentences like the ones above stop reading as marketing noise and start reading as information you can act on.

The phrasebook effect​

Analogy: imagine you land in a country where you don't speak the local language. You don't need fluency to get through your trip. You need about thirty words — numbers, directions, a handful of menu items — and suddenly the signs, the menu, and the station announcements stop being noise and start being information. You still can't hold a philosophical debate in that language, and you don't need to. Thirty words get you most of what you actually need.

The vocabulary of modern AI works the same way. You don't need a machine-learning degree to follow a product launch, a colleague's question in a meeting, or a vendor's pitch deck. You need about ten words, used precisely, and this lesson is your phrasebook.

Where these words live on the map​

Module 1 placed ChatGPT, Claude, and AI agents on one map: Machine Learning → Deep Learning → Large Language Models, each region narrower than the one before it — with Agents sitting outside that nesting, as a separate architecture wrapped around an LLM, not a fourth, narrower region inside it. Every term below lives somewhere specific on that map: five describe what's happening inside the model (tokens, parameters, embeddings, context window, hallucination), one describes a pattern wrapped on top (RAG), two belong to the agent architecture (agent, tool-calling), and two — eval and guardrail — apply no matter which region you're looking at, because both are checks you run on output, not properties of the model or the architecture itself.

Keep this shape in mind as you read the ten definitions below — it's the skeleton the deep dive will fill in with a lot more detail.

The ten terms​

Tokens​

A token is the small chunk of text a model actually reads and writes — sometimes a whole word, sometimes a fragment of one. It's also the unit every provider bills you on: a release note that says "2.3 million tokens processed this month" is describing a bill, not a capability. Why you care: when a colleague says an answer "cost more tokens than expected," they mean the request was more expensive, not that it was more accurate.

Parameters​

Parameters are the numbers a model learned during training — the actual, tunable values that encode everything it "knows how to do." A model described as "70 billion parameters" has 70 billion of these numbers. Why you care: parameter count is a rough proxy for a model's capacity and its compute cost — a vendor bragging about size is telling you something about quality and something about your hosting bill at the same time.

Embeddings​

An embedding is a piece of text (or an image, or a product) converted into a list of numbers — a point in space — chosen so that similar meanings end up close together and unrelated meanings end up far apart. Why you care: this is the mechanism behind "customers who bought this also bought that" and behind every search box that finds the right document even when you typed different words than the ones in it.

Context window​

The context window is the maximum amount of text — measured in tokens — a model can consider at once: your prompt, the conversation so far, any retrieved documents, all of it added together. It is not the model's memory of past conversations; once a conversation grows past this limit, the oldest parts fall out of view. Why you care: "the assistant forgot what I told it earlier" is almost always a context-window problem, not a training problem.

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation)​

You met RAG briefly in Module 1: instead of answering only from what it learned in training, the model first retrieves relevant text from a source you control, then writes its answer grounded in that text. Why you care: "grounded in your own documents" on a vendor's pitch deck is a RAG claim — worth asking what the assistant does when the retrieval step finds nothing relevant.

Agent​

An agent is an LLM wrapped with tools it can call, some form of memory, and a loop that lets it take more than one step before handing control back to you. A friendly persona bolted onto a chatbot does not make it an agent — what matters is whether it acts across multiple steps on its own. Why you care: "autonomous agent" in a pitch means something specific and testable, not just "a more advanced chatbot."

Tool-calling​

Tool-calling is the mechanism that makes an agent possible: instead of answering directly, the model can return a structured request to run a specific function — check a database, call an API, run a calculation — and your code executes it and hands the result back. Why you care: when an agent "does something wrong," tool-calling is usually where to look first — did it call the right tool, with the right arguments?

Eval​

An eval is a structured test that scores a model's or a system's answers against a rubric, usually run against a fixed set of real or representative questions before a change ships. Why you care: "we ran this against our eval suite" is the AI-era version of "we ran the test suite" — it's how a team knows a change is actually better, not just different.

Guardrail​

A guardrail is a rule enforced around a model or agent to stop it from doing something specific — repeating sensitive data back, calling a destructive tool, answering outside an allowed topic. Why you care: a guardrail is a constraint you can point to and test, unlike "the model is trained to be safe," which is a hope, not a control.

Hallucination​

A hallucination is a model stating something false with the same confidence as something true — inventing a citation, a fact, or a number with no signal that it's guessing. It is not the same as being wrong because of bad training data; a model can hallucinate even when its training data was accurate, because generating fluent text and generating true text are different skills. Why you care: this is the single risk every eval and guardrail in this lesson ultimately exists to catch.

The fastest way to sound like you know this field

Next time you read a release note or sit in a vendor call, count how many of these ten words show up. Most AI pitches use four or five of them, loosely. Being the person in the room who can ask "which retrieval step, over which documents?" or "what does your eval suite actually measure?" is most of the value of this lesson.
